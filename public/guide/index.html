Quick start

The Calculator tab opens on an example 30-06 load, so you're looking at a real answer before you type anything. To get your answer: pick your ammo, check your zero, then read the sentence at the top of the results. Four moves:

1

Pick your round

Search the catalog by caliber, manufacturer, or bullet name — or narrow first with the optional Caliber/Manufacturer filter chips — to pull real muzzle velocity, weight, and BC. Or type your own numbers for a handload. A catalog pick locks into one summary line ("172gr @ 2825 fps · G7 0.265") with an "Edit numbers" link if you need to change it.

2

Check your zero

Sight height over bore and zero range (both under "The sights," open from the start) describe your rifle — the catalog can't fill these in for you. Your target's vitals radius lives just below, folded until you want it.

3

Read the sentence at the top

A plain-English line leads the results: "Zeroed at 200 yd, at 300 yd your bullet lands 7.4 in (2.4 MOA) below where you aimed… To hit your aim point, hold up 2.4 MOA." Pick any distance from its dropdown. The speed, energy, and height tiles under it, the chart, and the table all follow.

4

Name and save it

The name field suggests one for you (from the catalog pick, or from grains/velocity/BC for a custom load) — keep it or rename it. After you save, a "✓ Saved" line tells you where it went: "Or open one of your saved loads," also used by Compare and Optimal Zero, so you never retype the same load twice.

On your very first visit a short "New here?" note repeats that path; tap "Got it" and it doesn't come back.

The seven tools

One tab per question. Nothing here is sequential — jump straight to whichever answers what you actually walked in asking.

CA

Calculator

The main tool — one load, one full trajectory, zeroed at a distance you choose.

  • Leads with a plain-English "answer line": where your bullet lands at the distance you pick, and what to hold to hit your aim point — including a left/right hold once wind is in.
  • Chart shows drop from your line of sight, not the bore — matches what you'd actually see through a scope.
  • Chart overlays: Vitals Zero band and Optimal Sight-in are on by default; Leupold BAS is opt-in.
  • Range table toggles MOA/MIL columns; "Print dope chart" builds a clean printable card.
  • An "Advanced" section (below Wind, collapsed even on first visit) adds spin drift and Coriolis — twist rate, bullet length/diameter, and latitude, all typed in by hand.
Watch forSight height, vitals radius, and the air fields are your shared rig — a drift bar offers to save or reset them the moment you change one.
CM

Compare

Overlays two or more saved loads on one chart and one table, so different rounds or rifles actually stack up against each other.

  • Needs at least one saved load from Calculator first — or add straight from the catalog.
  • "Compare at" sets the one distance the table reads out at.
  • Draws one Vitals band per distinct radius among the loads you've picked.
Watch forIf a load's own chart doesn't reach your "Compare at" distance, its row says so and its line just stops — that's correct, not broken.
OZ

Optimal Zero

For a whole list of rounds at once, finds the zero that keeps you inside the vitals for the longest possible stretch.

  • One shared rig on the left applies to every round on the right — the same physical setup, compared across ammo.
  • Surfaces both a practical near zero and the true optimal zero.
  • Add rounds from your saved loads or the catalog picker; remove any row.
Watch forThis rig is the same one Calculator uses — change it in either tab and the other picks it up.
RC

Recoil

Compares Free Recoil Energy across rifle-and-cartridge setups, straight from SAAMI's own published formula.

  • Each setup gets its own rifle-plus-optics weight — that's the actual variable worth comparing.
  • Picking a cartridge auto-fills an estimated powder charge (always marked "est.").
  • Results show as a table and a live bar chart as you add or remove setups.
Watch forThis is free recoil energy, not what a rifle feels like to shoot — stock, pad, and muzzle device aren't modeled. See the FAQ.
BE

Bullet Energy

A quick multi-row muzzle-energy comparison — weight and velocity in, energy out, nothing saved.

  • Muzzle energy only (E = weight×velocity² / 450,437) — the instant it leaves the barrel, not what's left downrange after drag. Same formula the Calculator and Recoil already use.
  • Type your own numbers per row, or fill one from the catalog picker above the table.
  • A bar chart appears once two or more rows have both fields filled in.
Watch forSession-only — nothing here is saved. It's a scratch pad for quick comparisons, not another place your loads live.
HT

Handloader's Tools

A hub, not a single tool — kept off the main tab bar so it doesn't crowd things for everyone else. Opening it always lands on a card grid; today that grid holds one card.

  • BC from Chronograph — back-solve your bullet's real BC from your own muzzle velocity plus a downrange velocity or time-of-flight reading, instead of trusting the box's number.
  • Root-finds around this app's own trajectory solver — zero new physics, the same math the Calculator already relies on, just run in reverse.
  • Atmosphere defaults to standard (59°F / 29.92 inHg), collapsed but always shown — expand it if your chrono session wasn't at standard conditions.
Watch forA "chrono-measured" BC and a catalog's "derived BC" are deliberately different labels for opposite situations — chrono-measured is from your own real data (arguably higher confidence than a catalog figure), derived is this app's own approximation for a load whose manufacturer never published one at all.
HP

Help

In-app how-tos, an FAQ, and a direct line to send an idea or report something wrong.

  • Every tab links straight to its own section here — "How does {tab} work?" (on a phone that first link shortens to "How it works").
  • "Suggest an idea" opens a prefilled email — no account or form to fill out. On a phone it's tucked out of the top link row and lives only here in Help.
Watch forThis guide covers the same ground in more depth — the in-app Help tab is the faster lookup while you're mid-form.

Reading the chart & table

Every chart in the app draws the same convention, so once you can read one you can read all of them.

Dashed line — your line of sight. The bullet starts below it, rises through it at the near zero, and falls back through it at the far zero.
Open dot — a zero crossing (near or far).
Filled dot — max ordinate, the highest point above the line of sight (the results tile calls it "Peak height").
Brass shading — transonic band, Mach 1.2 down to 1.0, where drag gets unpredictable.
Ox shading — subsonic, past Mach 1.0.
Vitals band (on by default) — the span where impact stays within your vitals radius of point of aim.

Above the chart, the answer line says in one sentence where your bullet lands at the distance you pick and what to hold to hit your aim point — in MOA by default (MIL in Metric, or if only MIL is ticked). On a steep shot (5° or more, up or down) it says those numbers are for a level shot and gives the distance to hold for instead.

The range table underneath lists height, then a windage column once wind, spin drift, or Coriolis is actually in effect — the footnote below the chart always says which of the three, if any, are on — then velocity, energy, time, and Mach at every step distance you set. A legend line under the "Range" heading spells out the signs: a minus height is below where you aimed, and an L or R windage is the side the bullet lands on. Turn on the MOA / MIL columns to get holdover in scope-click units instead of inches — each appears right beside the height or windage it converts. Print dope chart opens your browser's print dialog with a clean printable card — pick "Save as PDF" as the destination to keep one in your range bag.

Jargon, decoded

BCBallistic coefficient — how well a bullet resists drag. Higher means it sheds velocity more slowly downrange.
G1 / G7Two different reference drag shapes a BC can be measured against. G1 fits older flat-base bullets; G7 fits modern boat-tails more accurately. A BC has to be paired with the model it was published under — mixing them isn't a small error, it's the wrong calculation.
Muzzle velocityBullet speed the instant it leaves the barrel, in fps (or m/s in Metric).
Sight heightDistance from bore centerline to scope (or iron sight) centerline — typically 1.5–2.0in for a scope, about 0.8in for irons.
ZeroThe distance where your point of impact crosses your line of sight. A trajectory usually has two: a near zero (rising) and a far zero (falling back through).
Max ordinateThe highest point the bullet reaches above your line of sight — the top of the arc. The results tile calls it "Peak height."
Hold / holdoverAiming off on purpose to cancel drop or wind: "hold up 2.4 MOA and right 1.9 MOA" means aim that far above and to the right of where you want to hit. The answer line at the top of the Calculator's results works it out for you — the opposite direction from where the bullet lands.
Vitals radius / windowRadius is the half-width of the vital zone you're trying to stay inside (smaller for varmints, bigger for elk or moose). The window is how far out you can hold dead-on and still land inside it, given your current zero.
Optimal zeroThe zero distance that makes the vitals window as long as possible — the ballistics-nerd version of "how far can I just hold center."
MOA / MILAngular units scopes dial in. 1 MOA ≈ 1.047in per 100yd; 1 MIL = 3.6in per 100yd. Letting the table show holdover in these instead of raw inches matches what's printed on your turret. The answer line quotes your hold in MOA by default, in MIL if you're in Metric or only MIL is ticked.
Transonic / subsonicTransonic is the messy zone around Mach 1.2–1.0, where drag behavior gets less predictable as the bullet slows through the sound barrier. Subsonic is everything past Mach 1.0.
Station pressureThe actual, un-adjusted air pressure where you're standing — not the sea-level-corrected number a weather report usually gives you.
Shot angleSteep uphill or downhill shots carry less effective drop than the slant range suggests. On a steep shot the answer line says its numbers are for a level shot and names the shorter distance to hold for instead (the "rifleman's rule").
Twist rateHow fast your barrel's rifling spins the bullet, e.g. "1 turn in 8in." Needed, along with bullet length and diameter, to turn on spin drift — leave any one of the three blank and spin drift stays off.
Spin driftA sideways drift from the bullet's own gyroscopic spin, always toward the direction of the rifling twist. A standard closed-form approximation layered on the trajectory, not a full 3D solve — opt in under Calculator's "Advanced" section.
Coriolis effectA sideways (and slight vertical) drift from the earth's own rotation under the bullet during its time of flight — needs only your latitude to turn on, under Calculator's "Advanced" section. Matters far more at long range than short.
Free recoil energyExact physics — conservation of momentum and kinetic energy, from SAAMI's own published formula. Not the same as how a rifle feels to shoot, which also depends on stock, pad, and muzzle device.
Derived BCA load's own manufacturer didn't publish a BC, so one was back-solved from that manufacturer's own published downrange velocity numbers. Grounded in real data, but an approximation — always labeled, never presented as an official figure.
Chrono-measured BCA BC solved from your own chronograph data (Handloader's Tools → BC from Chronograph) — same root-finding method as a Derived BC, but deliberately labeled differently, since it's the opposite situation: your own real rifle and ammo, not the app's approximation for a manufacturer who published nothing. Arguably higher confidence than a catalog figure, not lower.

Where people get tripped up

"The target," "The air," "The wind," and "Spin drift & Coriolis" look empty on your first visit
They're not missing, just folded to a one-line summary — "The load," "The sights," and "The shot" start open, since those are the ones almost everyone needs, so the form doesn't read as a wall of fields the first time you land on Calculator. Click any header to open (or fold) a section, and the app remembers your choice on this device after that.
The sentence tells you where the bullet lands — then what to hold, the opposite way
"Your bullet lands 7.4 in below where you aimed… hold up 2.4 MOA" is one instruction in two halves: the first half describes the miss, the last sentence is what to dial or hold to fix it. A bullet that lands low or left needs a hold that's high or right. If you only read one part, read the last sentence.
G1 and G7 BCs aren't interchangeable
Switching the drag model without updating the BC value gives you a wrong answer, not a slightly-off one. The app warns before letting the switch go through — read that prompt rather than dismissing it.
A short line on the Compare chart isn't a bug
If a saved load's own charted range is shorter than your "Compare at" distance, its line simply stops where its own data ends and its table row says so. That's the load's actual scope, not something broken.
Saved loads live in your browser only — unless you sign in
Without an account, everything is local to the browser you're using. Clearing site data or switching devices starts you over. Sign in to sync saved loads and your rig across devices instead.
Opening a saved load doesn't touch your rig
A saved load carries its own sight height, zero, and conditions from whenever you saved it. Your shared "rig" (used by Calculator and Optimal Zero) only changes when you explicitly save or reset it.
"Fill from standard atmosphere" doesn't stack
It sets temperature and pressure directly from the altitude you entered — it doesn't add to whatever you already typed in those two fields. In the Calculator, altitude is the first field in "The air": type it, then tap the button.
Typing a different bullet weight doesn't change the BC
If you start from a catalog pick and change the bullet weight or drag model but leave the BC alone, the BC is still the original bullet's — so an amber "Heads up" note appears beside it naming the load it came from. Enter your own bullet's BC (it's on the box or the maker's site), or pick your ammo from the catalog instead. Changing only the velocity doesn't trigger the note, since the BC still fits.
Spin drift needs all three of its fields, not just one
Twist rate, bullet length, and bullet diameter all have to be filled in — leave any single one blank and spin drift stays off, silently, with no error. Coriolis is simpler: latitude alone turns it on.

Your data

Default

Signed out

Every saved load and your shared rig live only in this browser's local storage. Nothing is uploaded anywhere. Convenient and completely private — but tied to this one browser on this one device.

Optional

Signed in

An email + password account (confirm the email link to activate) syncs your saved loads and rig to the cloud, so the same setups follow you to another device or browser. Nothing beyond those two things is ever collected.

FAQ

Why is trajectory height measured from my line of sight, not the bore?

Because that's what you actually aim with and read on a reticle. The bullet starts one sight height below your line of sight, rises through it at the near zero, and falls back through it at the far zero — matching what you'd see at the range.

Does it support metric?

Yes — the Imperial/Metric toggle at the top applies everywhere. Everything is computed internally in imperial units and converted only for display and input, so switching back and forth never loses precision. In Metric the answer line quotes your hold in MIL rather than MOA.

Where does the estimated powder charge on the Recoil tab come from?

Manufacturers don't publish it for factory ammo. When a cartridge has known case capacity, the estimate comes from published case-capacity figures at a conservative ~90% load density — always labeled as an estimate, always overridable if you know your real charge weight.

Is Free Recoil Energy what a rifle "feels" like to shoot?

No. It's exact physics — conservation of momentum and kinetic energy, straight from SAAMI's published formula. What a rifle actually feels like also depends on stock geometry, recoil pads, and muzzle devices, none of which reduce to one formula — so "felt" recoil is deliberately not estimated.

How do spin drift and Coriolis work?

Both are standard closed-form approximations layered on top of the trajectory — not a full 3D solve of the bullet's spin or the earth's rotation, the same kind of established formula every serious ballistics calculator uses. Spin drift needs twist rate, bullet length, and bullet diameter (all three, or it stays off); Coriolis needs only your latitude. Both live under Calculator's "Advanced" section, collapsed by default since most shots never need them — they mostly matter past a few hundred yards. Off by default, on only if you fill them in. Checked against the same independent reference solver the core trajectory is (see below) — worst-case deviation is within 0.01in, tighter than the core trajectory's own 0.15in figure.

Is a "chrono-measured" BC the same kind of thing as a catalog's "derived BC"?

No — deliberately different words for opposite situations. "Derived BC" means a manufacturer never published a real figure, so this app approximated one from their other published data — lower confidence than a real published BC. A "chrono-measured" BC, from Handloader's Tools' BC from Chronograph, is empirical — solved from your own rifle, your own ammo, shot under your own conditions — arguably higher confidence than a generic catalog figure, not lower. Same root-finding method underneath either way, but the two labels intentionally don't share a word so they're never mistaken for each other.

What isn't modeled yet?

Aerodynamic jump and powder-temperature sensitivity aren't in the solver yet. Spin drift and the Coriolis effect are, as of Calculator's "Advanced" section (see above). The core trajectory — drag, wind deflection, atmosphere — is a full point-mass integration, not a simplified approximation, and is checked against an independent solver: worst-case deviation is under 1 fps on velocity and 0.15in on height, out to 1000yd.

Does the wind field use compass or clock directions?

Clock face, relative to you: 12 is straight into your face, 3 is your right cheek, 6 is at your back, 9 is your left cheek. Wind only counts once both a speed and a direction are filled in — enter just one and a note under the fields (and in the footer) says so; leave both blank for a no-wind trajectory.
